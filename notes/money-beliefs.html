Money notes · With Namita

Some money beliefs arrive before the first salary.

Notice the rules you inherited before deciding which ones still help.

Long before you had a bank account of your own, you probably heard rules about money. Some were spoken directly. Others were taught by who paid the bills, who was asked before a purchase, or who stayed quiet when the conversation turned to investments. A rule can become familiar enough that it stops sounding like a rule. It starts sounding like how the world works.

Familiar does not always mean useful.

Name one belief

Take a sentence you recognize, such as “I’m not a numbers person” or “Someone else will know better.” You do not have to reject it immediately. Write down where you learned it and whether it describes every situation you face today.

Look for a specific limit

A woman may carefully compare school fees, negotiate a work budget, and still believe she is incapable of understanding an investment statement. That does not mean those tasks are identical. It means the belief may be broader than the evidence. She can already learn unfamiliar information, compare options, and ask for clarification in parts of her life.

Use a believable replacement

“I am brilliant with money” may feel impossible to believe. “I can learn to understand one statement” is smaller and testable. It does not erase a difficult experience or remove real constraints. It gives you an action you can observe.

Try the workbook

In the Money Reset Workbook, complete the prompts about what you learned and which belief you are ready to question. Keep the answers private. A belief exercise does not solve financial control, a cash shortfall, or an unsafe relationship. Those may require support beyond a worksheet.

For now, choose the sentence you want to examine, rather than trying to rewrite everything you believe about money.
