About

I did not inherit it, and I did not marry it. I built it.

Fifteen years in corporate. A salary, a savings habit, and a lot of patience. Enough of a corpus to walk away from a job I no longer wanted, three years ago, and not go back.

The short version

I spent fifteen years working in corporate India. Good roles, long hours, the usual. Somewhere in the middle of it I started paying real attention to my money, not because I was passionate about finance but because I was tired.

Tired of the feeling that my life belonged to a calendar someone else controlled. Tired of doing the maths in my head about how many months I could survive if I quit, and always getting a small number.

So I made the number bigger. Slowly, month after month, for years. Nothing clever. I earned, I kept my costs sane, I invested the difference into things I understood, and I did not touch it when it fell.

Three years ago the number got big enough. I resigned. Since then I have managed my own capital full time, and I answer to nobody about how I spend a Tuesday.

The part that made me start teaching

After I quit, women started asking me how I did it. Smart women. Women running teams, closing deals, holding families together.

And then the same sentence kept coming up, in different words.

My husband handles all that. I do not really know what we have.

Or, my father set it up. Or, there is a relationship manager at the bank who calls, and I sign what he sends.

These were not helpless women. They were women who had simply never been taught, and had quietly decided that money was a subject for other people. Many of them earned more than the person making their money decisions.

I found that genuinely upsetting, and I could not unsee it. So I started teaching.

What I believe

  • Not knowing is a risk too. People treat investing as risky and cash as safe. Handing your future to someone else and not understanding it is its own kind of risk, and it usually shows up at the worst possible moment.
  • Money is not the point. Choice is the point. Money is only the thing that buys choice, and choice is what changes how a woman lives.
  • Boring wins. The people who do well over twenty years are almost never the clever ones. They are the ones who kept it simple and did not stop.
  • Basic questions are the good ones. If a finance person makes you feel stupid for asking, the problem is the person, not the question.
  • You should be able to explain what you own. If you cannot explain it in one sentence to a friend, you probably should not be in it.

What I will not do

I want this in writing, on my own website, so there is no confusion.

  • I will not tell you which share, fund or policy to buy.
  • I will not manage your money, hold your money, or place trades for you.
  • I do not take commission, referral fees or sponsorship from any financial product or platform.
  • I am not registered with SEBI as an investment adviser or a research analyst, so I do not give personal investment advice.
  • I will never tell you what a return is going to be, because I do not know and neither does anybody else.

What I do is teach. How the instruments work, how markets behave, how tax works, what the common traps are, and how to make a decision on your own. Everything I earn comes from the people I teach, which is the only arrangement that keeps my incentives honest.

Outside the spreadsheets

I read more than I should, walk every morning, and have strong opinions about tea. I still track my own money on a single sheet I built years ago, because complicated systems are the ones you stop using.
